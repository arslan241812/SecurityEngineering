### Task 1: Lock-ins

In this week's course material, you might have heard about concept of lock-ins, often differentiated into **technological** and **vendor lock-ins**. Provide a brief summary of each type of lock-in. Feel free to share any personal insights you may have regarding your own experiences with these lock-ins. Additionally, discuss the potential costs associated with breaking free from these lock-ins and the advantages and disadvantages of remaining within them.

Aim for around 150-200 words in your answer and list sources used.
Technological Lock-in occurs when a system is built on a specific, proprietary technology that is difficult to replace. The investment in skills, processes, and infrastructure creates high switching costs. This is quite often from proprietary formats, tightly integrated systems, or legacy infrastructure that’s incompatible with newer alternatives.
Vendor Lock-in is on the other hand, refers to where dependence is on a specific company's ecosystem,  such as cloud platforms or software suites where switching vendors would require costly migration, retraining, or data conversion.
From my own experience, I’ve seen users struggle with cloud ecosystems like AWS or Azure, where deep integration offers convenience but limits flexibility. Breaking free can involve re-engineering systems, renegotiating contracts, and enduring downtime.
Costs of escaping lock-in include migration expenses, data loss or corruption risks, operational delays, staff retraining while its beneficial in terms of seamless integration, optimized performance, vendor support.

### Task2: Security Psychology

Familiarize yourself with definitions for following; cognitive-, behavioral- and social psychology, course material and search engines are your friends!
using one or multiple of them, explain your rationale for following phenomena:
- why are phishing attacks effective enough to be widespread practice?
  These attacks are widespread practice because phishing exploits cognitive limits such as mental shortcuts that enable fast decision making but can lead to errors. Attackers create urgent, attention-grabbing cues that hijack limited attentional resources and trigger fast decision making rather than careful analysis. Behavioral factors like  small immediate rewards (access to content, relief from worry) and lack of immediate punishment make trigger risky clicks as learned behavior. Social influences amplify success when messages appear to come from trusted colleagues or authority figures, which leverages conformity and obedience to perceived social signals.
- why social engineering works on people?
  Social engineering is highly  effective due to principles of social psychology, specifically the ingrained human tendency to comply with authority and follow social norms. People are conditioned from childhood to respect and obey authority figures (e.g., "IT Support," "Your Bank's Security Team"). Attackers impersonate these roles to manufacture legitimacy. cybersecurity behavior is deeply social because interventions that ignore group norms or interpersonal dynamics fail, while attacks that mirror workplace communication are highly effective
- why many people have hard time using  passwords in secure way?
- This is a problem of cognitive psychology and behavioral psychology that humans have limited memory capacity and prefer low-effort strategies, so they reuse memorable passwords or simplify rules to reduce load. From a behavioral perspective, the negative consequence of a potential future hack is abstract and distant, while the immediate "punishment" of forgetting a complex password and getting locked out of an account is certain and frustrating. Therefore, the immediate need of convenience forces the insecure behavior.
- why it is so easy to spread malware?
  Malware spread is so easy due to the social psychology and cognitive behaviour. Many malware campaigns rely on social proof the idea that if others are doing something, it must be safe or correct. For example, malware masquerades as a popular video or a trending topic, tricking users into believing it's legitimate because it appears many others are engaging with it. Social networks and shared devices create transmission pathways like peer-to-peer trust and common software dependencies (same platforms, libraries, plugins) create technical and social homophily that speeds spread.

### Task3A: Intellectual Rights

Companies prioritize safeguarding their assets, encompassing both tangible and intangible aspects. This protection involves utilizing legal mechanisms like: 

- Intellectual Property (IP)
Intellectual Property is the umbrella concept covering creations of the mind that the law can protect so creators can control and benefit from their work.
usecase: The entire business model of a company like The Walt Disney Company is built on IP.
  
- Copyright
A legal right that grants the creator of an original work (e.g., literature, art, music, software) exclusive rights to its use and distribution for a limited time.
Usecase: J.K. Rowling holds the copyright to the Harry Potter books.
  
- Patent
A government granted right that gives an inventor exclusive rights to a process, design, or invention for a limited period (usually 20 years) in exchange for public disclosure.
Usecase: Pfizer holds patents for its drugs, like Viagra.
  
- Trademark
A recognizable sign, design, or expression (e.g., a logo, brand name, slogan) that identifies products or services from a particular source and distinguishes them from others.
Usecase: The Nike "Swoosh" logo and the phrase "Just Do It" are trademarks.
  
- Non-Disclosure Agreements (NDAs)
A legally binding contract that establishes a confidential relationship. The signatory party agrees not to disclose any information covered by the agreement.
Usecase: When Apple discusses a new iPhone's design and features with a manufacturing partner, they require employees at that partner company to sign NDAs to prevent leaks before the official launch.

Along with employing technologies such as:

- Watermarks
A recognizable image, pattern, or code embedded into a digital or physical asset to identify its ownership and authenticate its originality. It can be visible or invisible.
Usecase: Stock photo websites like Getty Images embed visible watermarks on low-resolution preview images. Once purchased, the customer receives a clean, high-resolution copy without the watermark.
- Software licenses
A legal instrument that governs the use or redistribution of software. It defines the rights of the user (e.g., installation, number of users) and is a key method for enforcing copyright.
Usecase: Microsoft Office uses a proprietary software license. When you purchase it, you are buying a license to use the software under specific terms, not owning the software itself.
- Digital rights management (DRM)
A set of access control technologies used to restrict the use of proprietary hardware and copyrighted digital content. It prevents unauthorized redistribution and limits how consumers can copy the content they've purchased.
Usecase:Netflix and Spotify use DRM to encrypt their streams.
- Software protection dongles
A small hardware device that plugs into a computer (often via USB) and acts as a key for a specific software application. The software will only run if the dongle is present.
Usecase: SOLIDWORKS and audio workstations (e.g., Pro Tools) often use dongles. This allows licensed software to be easily moved between different computers while effectively preventing piracy.

Please provide a brief explanation of each mentioned safeguarding method and cite any known use cases for each. Notice that subjects have some overlap, feel free to format your answer in a convenient way

### Task3B: Freedom of Information

Other side of the coin, information wants to be free. Pick 2 safeguarding methods from previous part and Investigate and present real-world instances where the aforementioned safeguarding methods were bypassed, highlighting entities involved and the rationale behind their actions. Additionally, report on how they justify these circumventions.

1. Patent circumvention
Patent protection grants inventors exclusive rights to their inventions. Circumvention typically involves unauthorized use or replication of patented technology, often justified by claims of innovation, affordability, or public interest.Circumventers often claim they are enabling compatibility or improving technology. In pharmaceuticals, circumvention is justified as necessary for public health and access to essential goods.Some entities exploit ambiguities in laws by arguing that replication is acutually not equal to circumvention to avoid penalties.
E.g: Pharmaceutical Patent Evergreening (AstraZeneca vs. Ranbaxy)
AstraZeneca hold patents for the acid reflux drug Nexium, engaging in "evergreening" with minor modifications to extend patent life while Ranbaxy produced generic versions of Nexium, arguing AstraZeneca's patents were invalidated by evergreening. Ranbaxy sought to provide affordable generic medicines, claiming AstraZeneca abused patent laws to delay generic competition and maintain high prices.Ranbaxy framed its actions as serving public health interests by improving access to essential drugs. They argued that evergreening practices harmed consumers and violated the balance between innovation and accessibility. Courts initially sided with AstraZeneca, but the case intensified debates on pharmaceutical patent reforms. It underscored the need to prevent evergreening while respecting legitimate patent rights.
3. Software Protection Dongles:
Dongles are hardware devices that must be connected to a computer to authorize software use. They are common in high-value software like CAD tools, audio workstations, and medical systems.
E.g: SolidWorks Dongle Emulation (2010s)
SolidWorks (Dassault Systèmes) used hardware dongles (e.g., Sentinel HASP) to protect its CAD software while Piracy groups developed emulators like "MultiKey" or "Sentinel LM Emulator" to mimic dongle functionality without physical hardware.Users sought to avoid the cost of licensing (which could exceed $10,000 per seat) or the inconvenience of physical dongles, especially in remote work scenarios. Emulators allowed unauthorized copies to run by simulating dongle responses.Pirates argued that dongles were obstructive for legitimate users (e.g., losing dongles or needing multiple instances). Some claimed to use cracked software for educational purposes or short-term projects. At the end Dassault Systèmes enhanced its protection with encryption and anti-debugging measures, but emulators persisted. The company also shifted toward cloud-based licensing to reduce dongle dependence.
